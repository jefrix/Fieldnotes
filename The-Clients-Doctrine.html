The Client's
Doctrine

On the alliance mechanic Israel has been running for a decade, made legible this week by a rejected chair at a board its prime minister still sits on.

Published August 10, 2026
Reading time ~20 minutes
Filed under Geopolitics / Alliances
Part of Volume Three

On Sunday, August 9, 2026, at the start of a weekly Israeli cabinet meeting, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu made a statement that would be, without prior context, difficult to parse. He said: "I want to clarify this: Israel rejects the 15-point document. I have heard people saying, 'You didn't say that.' So here I am saying it again." The 15-point document in question is the roadmap for postwar Gaza issued on July 31 by the U.S.-backed Board of Peace. Prime Minister Netanyahu accepted an invitation to join that Board on January 21, 2026, and remains a member of it as of this writing.

What Netanyahu was announcing, in effect, was that he as a member of the Board of Peace was rejecting the plan of the Board of Peace. He would not resign his membership. He would not withdraw Israel's participation in the framework. He would simply reject the plan the framework had produced, while continuing to occupy the seat that framework had given him.

This dispatch is about why this move is completely coherent within the strategic logic of the actor performing it, why the American political system cannot easily punish it, and what its widespread visibility signals for the alliance system the United States has maintained since 1945.

Chapter I

The Mechanic

The behavior Netanyahu displayed on Sunday is not an anomaly. It is the operational form of a specific mechanic that Israeli statecraft has been running with increasing openness since roughly 2015. The mechanic has three steps.

Accept the frame. When the American administration proposes a diplomatic structure, coalition, or normalization framework, Israel accepts membership. The Board of Peace acceptance in January is the canonical example; the Abraham Accords participation, the various UN-adjacent formats, the initial engagement with the JCPOA process before the Trump 1 withdrawal, and the executive-committee negotiations of the Board itself all follow the same pattern. Acceptance costs Israel nothing at the moment of acceptance; it delivers the association benefit (visible partnership with American initiatives) immediately.

Extract the association benefit. Once inside the frame, Israel gains legibility in the American political conversation as a partner. This provides diplomatic cover for adjacent operations, access to information about the framework's internal deliberations, and the ability to shape the framework's substantive content from the inside. During Trump 1, the mechanic delivered the embassy move to Jerusalem, U.S. recognition of the Golan annexation, U.S. withdrawal from the JCPOA, and the Abraham Accords normalization framework, all of which Israel had been unable to secure through decades of straight-line advocacy.

Reverse the substantive commitment. When the framework matures to the point of imposing actual costs (Gaza withdrawal, Palestinian statehood, Hezbollah negotiations, constraints on preemptive action), Israel repudiates the specific commitment while retaining the general membership. The repudiation is typically justified by a maximalist condition no counterparty can plausibly meet: "genuine disarmament", "verifiable dismantlement", "complete cessation of hostile activity". The condition is not a negotiating position. It is a mechanism for permanent deferral, which allows Israel to continue extracting the association benefit indefinitely while never incurring the substantive cost.

The mechanic works because at no step does Israel do anything that requires an American response. Membership is voluntary. Association benefit is a byproduct of American priorities. Reversal is a policy statement, not a treaty violation. The American administration facing the reversal cannot easily punish it without breaking the general association, which the administration also needs for its own domestic political reasons.

Chapter II

The Doctrine with Adversaries

The mechanic-with-allies exists inside a specific strategic context, and that context is worth describing briefly because it shapes what allies get out of the alliance and what they don't.

Post-October 7, 2023, Israel has visibly shifted from what analysts call the pre-Oct-7 "management by deterrence" doctrine to what former Mossad director Tamir Pardo has publicly termed the "keep all fronts burning" doctrine. Under management, Israel used limited strikes with deterrent intent, relied on Iron Dome to absorb retaliation, avoided targeting state actors directly, and accepted U.S.-imposed ceasefires when it had extracted enough damage. Post-Oct-7, all four of those constraints are gone. Israel now conducts sustained, expanding operations across seven fronts: Iran, Lebanon, Syria, Iraq, Yemen, Gaza, and the West Bank. State actors are targets, and the killing of Khamenei and strikes on the Iranian presidential compound are the clearest examples. Buffer zones have been physically established through military occupation in Gaza (roughly 70 percent of the territory as of this writing), southern Lebanon (Ali al-Taher ridge operations continuing this week), and parts of Syria. Ceasefires are entered when tactically convenient and violated when they cease to serve.

The doctrinal frame is the Begin Doctrine (1981), which held that Israel would preemptively destroy emerging existential threats regardless of international opinion or ally coordination. The current government has extended it in two directions: first, downward, to include non-existential threats treated as if existential; second, geographically, to include any actor within the "Axis of Resistance" network regardless of whether that actor has itself directly struck Israel. Israel's own security establishment recorded a 63 percent rise in West Bank settler "nationalistic crime" during the first half of 2026, classified by those same security bodies as terrorism. That rise is not incidental to the doctrine; it is the doctrine expressing itself through non-state actors whose behavior the government tolerates because those actors are also part of its political coalition.

The result is a state in continuous kinetic action against a variable set of adversaries, with the specific adversary at any given moment chosen for its immediate tactical opportunity rather than for any coherent regional plan. This is not a strategic failure. It is a strategic feature, because the continuous action preserves the political conditions under which the client-doctrine mechanic can continue to function. Peace with any single adversary would create pressure for peace with the others, which would in turn create pressure for domestic political accountability the current coalition cannot survive.

Chapter III

The Doctrine with Allies

Israel does not treat its allies as partners in the ordinary sense. It treats them as instruments: sometimes cooperative, sometimes coercive, but always evaluated against a single metric, which is what can be extracted from the relationship now given the current cost of maintaining it. This is not unusual behavior for a state operating from a position of structural insecurity. What is unusual is the openness with which the behavior is now conducted, and the extent to which it has become institutionalized rather than being a temporary posture of a particular government.

Three examples this week make the mechanic concrete.

The Trump non-response. Asked in the Oval Office today for a response to Netanyahu's rejection of the 15-point plan, Trump said: "I have a response. I put it out today on Truth — a good response." No such statement had been posted at the time of the reporting. Trump then insisted his relationship with Netanyahu is "very good." The response is not a response. It is the American president publicly maintaining the fiction that the alliance is functioning as designed, because the alternative (publicly acknowledging that the alliance is being conducted on the client's terms) is domestically unaffordable.

The operational-diplomatic split. The same day Netanyahu rejected the Board of Peace plan, IDF Chief of Staff Lt. Gen. Eyal Zamir met with U.S. Central Command chief Adm. Brad Cooper in Israel. Military coordination continues at the operational level while diplomatic coordination breaks down at the political level. This is not a bug. It is the specific mechanic by which the alliance is now conducted: the operational tier gives the client the material support it needs (weapons, intelligence, defensive integration), the political tier is left to bilateral rhetorical management, and the two tiers are increasingly decoupled. The decoupling is the technology that permits the client-doctrine to function.

The electoral instrumentalization. Netanyahu is seating on the Likud slate for the October 27 election a candidate, Oren Dobronsky, who recently called Trump a "morally corrupt piece of shit" over the Iran MOU. Dobronsky is now attempting damage control by calling Trump Israel's "greatest friend" in the White House. Read this carefully. A sitting Israeli prime minister is deliberately elevating a candidate whose public comments about the sitting American president would ordinarily be disqualifying, three months before an election in which his coalition is trailing. The coalition math tells you why: Trump-critical voters on the Israeli right exist in numbers Netanyahu needs. He is signaling to his base that friction with Washington is politically acceptable, even valuable. That is a deliberate positioning against the alliance for domestic electoral purposes.

The three examples share a structure. In each, Israel is doing something that in a symmetrical alliance would be understood as violation of trust. In each, the American response is muted, delayed, or entirely absent. In each, the operational infrastructure of the alliance continues functioning underneath. The client is discovering, in public, how much can be done without triggering the guarantor's exit. The answer, so far, is: a very great deal.

Chapter IV

Netanyahu Specifically, and Israel Generally

The question worth being precise about is how much of this is Israel and how much is Netanyahu.

The mechanic itself is genuinely Israeli, deep in the strategic culture, and would persist under a different prime minister. The Begin doctrine, the strong-horse framing, and the willingness to act unilaterally against allies' preferences predate Netanyahu and would survive him. The Abraham Accords were negotiated by a Netanyahu government but the underlying logic (bypassing the Palestinian question to normalize with Sunni Arab states) reflects a broader Israeli consensus.

What is Netanyahu-specific is the intensity, the electoral timing, and the personal stakes. He is on trial for corruption. He faces an October 27 election with his coalition trailing in polls. His government survives only with support from Smotrich, Ben-Gvir, and the settler right, all of whom would collapse the coalition if he agreed to any framework involving genuine Gaza withdrawal or Palestinian sovereignty. The continued war is not a byproduct of his political situation; it is the political situation. Peace, in any form the international community would recognize, would end his coalition, expose him to his corruption trial without the shield of wartime leadership, and likely end his political career. He has strong personal reasons to prevent that outcome.

Senior Israeli security professionals have been openly critical. Pardo and former senior Peres advisor Nimrod Novik have publicly named the doctrine as "cynical." That is unusual; former Mossad directors rarely break with a sitting government publicly. It suggests that a meaningful faction of the Israeli security establishment believes the current MO is Netanyahu's personal survival strategy rather than a defensible national one, and it opens the possibility that a successor government would revert to something closer to the pre-2023 management doctrine.

But (and this is the critical qualification) even a successor government would inherit the mechanic. The extraction-without-submission pattern is available to whoever occupies the prime minister's chair, and any Israeli government facing a similar combination of American diplomatic pressure and unresolved regional threats would have strong incentives to reach for the same tool. Netanyahu did not invent this. He has industrialized it.

Chapter V

Why the Moment Matters

The scaled-up client mechanic would be an Israel story except for one thing: every other American ally is watching.

South Korea is watching. The Lee government's foreign policy of "stable ties with the PRC" while continuing to host USFK and coordinate on Taiwan-contingency infrastructure is a structurally analogous client-doctrine posture, adapted to a different geometry. Japan is watching. Its willingness to conduct the July 31 coordinated yen intervention with the U.S. Treasury while continuing to independently negotiate with China on rare earths and semiconductor supply chains is another instantiation. India is watching most closely. The June 2026 pattern of accepting Trump-administration constraints on Russian oil purchases and Chabahar port investment while continuing to maintain BRICS chairmanship-level engagement with Moscow and Tehran is the client mechanic in action.

The observation these allies are performing is simple. It asks how much can be extracted from the American alliance without triggering the American exit. The answer they are seeing in Israel's case (a great deal, possibly indefinitely) will shape their own future behavior. This is the alliance-side mirror of moral hazard. Once one client demonstrates that the association benefit can be extracted while the substantive commitment is repudiated, every other client faces the choice of whether to follow the same script.

If they do, the alliance system the United States has maintained since 1945 continues to exist in name while progressively hollowing out in substance. If they don't, Israel remains an anomalous case whose behavior is possible only because of specific American domestic political factors (evangelical Republican base commitment, the influence of pro-Israel advocacy organizations, historical guilt) that other clients cannot replicate. The interesting question is which trajectory the next twenty-four months settle into, and the answer depends less on any particular ally's decisions than on whether the American political system produces a countervailing response.

The signal to watch is not any specific policy statement. It is whether the American administration begins imposing costs on clients that repudiate substantive commitments. So far, there is no evidence of this. Trump's non-response today is the perfect indicator: publicly the relationship is affirmed, substantively the client is unpunished, operationally the material support continues. This is the equilibrium the client-doctrine is designed for, and it is holding.

Chapter VI

What Would Falsify

The reading has weaknesses, and it is worth naming them.

If Netanyahu loses the October 27 election and a successor government restores the Board of Peace framework and completes Gaza-withdrawal talks with Hamas, the argument that the client-doctrine has become structural weakens considerably. It becomes a story about a specific prime minister's survival strategy rather than a system feature. This is the scenario I would consider most likely to reset the analysis, and it is a live possibility given current polling.

If the Trump administration imposes visible costs (reducing arms deliveries, withdrawing intelligence cooperation, publicly withholding support at UN votes), the mechanic breaks down and the client-doctrine analysis needs to be revisited. This is not currently happening but could happen, particularly if American public opinion on Israel deteriorates further and the political calculus for the White House shifts.

If other U.S. allies visibly decline to adopt similar mechanics (if India recommits to strategic autonomy under real American pressure, if Japan avoids the yen-intervention path in future crises, if Korea publicly commits to the Taiwan-defense position), the client-doctrine remains Israel-specific rather than becoming a system feature.

If the Iran war produces a settlement that reduces Israel's regional threat environment enough to enable a genuine diplomatic phase, the political conditions for continuous kinetic action degrade, and the mechanic loses one of its enabling variables.

These are all real objections. What holds the reading up despite them is that the mechanic has been operating in the same form for at least a decade, has survived multiple American administrations of different parties and orientations, and has now become visible enough that other clients cannot fail to observe it. Whether they act on the observation is the falsifiable question. Whether the observation exists is not.

Chapter VII

 

The Interceptor Standard argued that reliability changed units, from reserve to production rate. The Underwriter State argued that the American state has quietly become an equity holder in strategic industry. The Sovereign Bid argued that the state has become a market-maker of last resort in categories private capital cannot clear. All three dispatches were about the guarantor.

The Client's Doctrine is about the guarantee's counterparty. The Board of Peace member who rejects the Board's plan is doing, in international politics, exactly what a firm does when it accepts a government equity stake while continuing to run its business in ways the government cannot easily control. The extraction pattern is symmetric on both sides of the guarantor-client relationship. What the American state does when it becomes an equity holder in Intel is what Israel does when it accepts membership in the Board of Peace. What Intel does when it optimizes for private returns while banking on federal protection is what Israel does when it repudiates the substantive commitment while retaining the alliance.

Neither party is behaving badly on its own terms. Each is optimizing rationally within an incentive structure that neither designed. The structure permits (indeed rewards) the mechanic. Until the structure is changed, the behavior will continue.

The Board of Peace exists. The member has rejected the plan. He remains the member. Tomorrow the operational coordination continues. The chair is filled and empty at the same time, and no one will move to change the seating arrangement, because no one can afford to.

That is the client's doctrine, and it is the negotiation of every important alliance the United States now maintains.
