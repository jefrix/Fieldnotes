The Load-Bearing
Rating

Three agencies call Oracle investment grade. The credit market prices it like a single-B company. A bank just sold a slice of an Oracle-linked loan for 85 cents on the dollar. On why formal standing outlasts belief, and on the creditors' decree that was written in advance.

Published November 22, 2026
Reading time ~16 minutes
Filed under Markets / History / Systems
Scores №16's prediction

On November 17, Bloomberg reported that HSBC had sold about $300 million of a loan it helped make to a data center in New Mexico. The project is called Jupiter. A unit of Blue Owl Capital is building it, the financing behind it totals $18 billion, and the tenant whose lease payments are supposed to repay all of it is Oracle. HSBC sold its slice in the mid-80s: roughly 85 cents for every dollar of face value, a loss of something like $45 million on a loan that was about a year old.

Banks sell loans all the time, and a single sale proves little. What makes this one worth a dispatch is that in the same month, by the formal reckoning of all three major rating agencies, the company standing behind that loan was investment grade. S&P rates Oracle BBB-. Moody's rates it Baa2. Fitch rates it BBB. Investment grade is the category the financial system reserves for borrowers it considers safe enough for pension funds, insurers, and the conservative mandates that hold most of the world's corporate debt.

Meanwhile the market that prices insurance against Oracle defaulting (the credit default swap market, where a buyer pays an annual fee to be made whole if the borrower fails) was charging around 300 basis points a year: about $300,000 annually to insure $10 million of Oracle debt. A year earlier the same insurance cost under 60 basis points. The financial press reported in early November that the new level sat just inside the range the market associates with single-B companies, four or five rungs below where the agencies place Oracle, deep in the territory once called junk.

So there are two prices for one promise. The agencies say Oracle will pay. The market says it might not. This dispatch is about that gap: why it exists, why it can persist much longer than either side's story would suggest, and what the history of such gaps says about how they close. The Collapse Signature research project has proposed treating the gap itself as a signature element, under the name formal standing outlasting market belief. This is the first public test of that proposal.

One caution belongs at the top, and it governs everything below. Nothing here forecasts that Oracle will default. The historical cases that make the gap interesting mostly did not end in sudden failure. They ended in something slower, and in some ways stranger.

Chapter I

Two Prices for One Promise

Begin with what the agencies actually said, because they did not say Oracle was safe. They said something narrower.

On July 9, S&P cut Oracle from BBB to BBB-, the lowest investment-grade rating, with a stable outlook. The numbers S&P published alongside the cut are not the numbers of a comfortable borrower. It forecast a free operating cash flow deficit of nearly $42 billion for the fiscal year ending May 2027, up from a forecast of $24 billion. It noted capital spending guidance of $90 billion to $95 billion for the same year, against its own earlier assumption of $60 billion. It counted roughly $260 billion of data center lease commitments due to begin between fiscal 2027 and 2029, and $638 billion of contracted future revenue, about half of it from a single customer, OpenAI. It put adjusted leverage in the mid-4x range, at the edge of its own downgrade threshold.

What held the rating up was not the cash flow. It was a pair of commitments. Oracle has said it intends to remain investment grade, and it has backed that statement with equity: a $5 billion mandatory convertible preferred issue in February and a planned $20 billion raise during 2026. S&P's rating, read closely, is a rating on management's willingness to dilute shareholders to protect creditors. In September, a Fortune analysis argued that S&P's own projections (free cash flow negative through 2027, debt rising more than fourfold between 2022 and 2028) did not support an investment-grade call at all. Moody's affirmed Baa2 that same month but attached a negative outlook. Fitch sits at BBB, stable.

The market's reading moved in one direction all year. Oracle's five-year swap traded near 58 basis points in October 2025, around 144 at the start of 2026, about 203 by late July, and between 295 and 315 by October 20. In the first week of November it held near 300. Nobody needs to argue that these figures are right to notice that they are a verdict, rendered daily by people with money at stake, and that the verdict differs from the agencies' by several letter grades.

Oracle is not the only company living in this gap. In the same week, Nvidia's swaps traded around 139 basis points, a level the trade publication IFR associated with double-B credit, against an actual rating of double-A. CoreWeave, the GPU cloud operator rated B+ by S&P, traded above 1,150 basis points after briefly spiking past 1,289. The pattern is general enough that IFR ran it as a headline: across the AI-linked names, the swap market is pricing ratings well below the ones the agencies assign.

The agencies did not say Oracle was safe. They said Oracle had promised to stay rated, and had shown it would sell stock to keep the promise. That is a rating on intentions, and the market is pricing the arithmetic. — Two prices for one promise
Chapter II

Why the Rating Holds

The easy explanation for the gap is that the agencies are slow, captured, or wrong. They have been all three before. The Senate committee that studied Enron found that the agencies kept the company investment grade until November 28, 2001, four days before it filed for bankruptcy, after its shares had collapsed toward a dollar, and concluded that despite their access to inside information they had been no faster than ordinary analysts. Every argument about rating agencies eventually returns to Enron.

But the easy explanation misses the more interesting one, which is structural, and which applies even to agencies that are doing their jobs honestly. The rating is not only an opinion about a borrower. It is a gate in the plumbing of the financial system, and moving it changes the thing it measures.

A large share of corporate debt is held by institutions that are permitted, by law or by mandate, to own only investment-grade bonds. When a borrower falls below BBB-, those holders must sell, all at once, into a market that knows they must sell. Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid made exactly this point about Oracle in October: a downgrade to junk could force investment-grade-only investors out of the bonds. The forced selling raises the borrower's cost of funding, which weakens its finances, which justifies the downgrade after the fact. The rating, in other words, is load-bearing. Withdrawing it is not a neutral act of measurement. It is itself a shock, and the agency knows that the shock will be attributed to it.

There is a second layer of insulation that rarely makes the headlines. Bloomberg's widely used bond indexes classify a bond by the middle of its three ratings rather than the lowest. Oracle's three ratings are BBB-, Baa2, and BBB; the middle one is investment grade with a notch to spare. For Oracle's bonds to drop out of the investment-grade indexes, two agencies would have to move, not one. The headline phrase "one notch above junk" is accurate about S&P and misleading about the system.

None of this makes the rating corrupt. It makes the rating sticky, for reasons any careful analyst would recognize. A borrower that will sell equity to protect its standing, in a system where withdrawing that standing triggers the very distress it predicts, is a borrower an agency has rational grounds to leave alone for as long as the commitments are being honored. The market has no such inhibition. A swap price can move fifty basis points in a morning without forcing anyone to sell anything.

This is the temporal-mismatch pattern in a form the series has not yet documented: not an institution too slow to see a threat, but an institution that can see it perfectly well and has structural reasons to move slowly, because its own movement is part of the threat. The 2005 downgrades of Ford and General Motors to junk are the useful reminder of how that tension usually resolves. The credit markets shook, the forced selling happened, and within months the episode looked like a blip. Ford came through without a bankruptcy; General Motors reached one four years later, in a different crisis with different causes. A painful repricing, not a sudden ruin, is the common outcome when a large borrower loses its standing. Enron is the exception everyone remembers because it is the exception.

◆ The signature, stated plainly

The Collapse Signature project's candidate variable is not the gap between ratings and prices; such gaps open and close constantly. It is the gap's persistence: a formal standing that continues to be honored by institutions long after the people with money at stake have stopped believing it, because withdrawing the standing would itself be destabilizing. The research question is what happens while the gap stays open. History has a precise answer, and it is not the one the word "collapse" suggests.

Chapter III

The Fifty-Three Percent Empire

In 1854, during the Crimean War, the Ottoman Empire took out its first foreign loan. It was a great power, a member of the European system its allies were fighting to preserve, and its standing in the councils of Europe was not in question. Over the next twenty years it borrowed repeatedly in London and Paris, and the market's verdict on that standing can be read in a single ratio. Of roughly 242 million Ottoman pounds borrowed in nominal terms between 1854 and 1874, the government actually received about 128 million. The rest disappeared into discounts, commissions, and the deep markdowns at which the bonds were issued. From the first loans, the market was pricing the empire at a little more than half of what its formal standing implied.

The standing held anyway, for two decades, because too much rested on it. By 1874 and 1875, annual debt charges exceeded half of the empire's regular revenue. In October 1875, the government issued what became known as the Decree of Ramadan and suspended payment on its debt. That was the moment the gap closed from the market's side. What followed is the part of the story that matters most for anyone reading Oracle's swap prices today.

The empire did not fall. It negotiated. In 1881, the Decree of Muharrem reduced the debt and created the Ottoman Public Debt Administration, an institution governed by the creditors, with a single Ottoman seat on its council. It was given the right to collect specific revenues directly: the state monopolies on salt and tobacco, and taxes on stamps, spirits, fishing, and silk, among others. At its peak it employed roughly 9,000 people, more than the Ottoman finance ministry. A foreign creditors' bureaucracy, larger than the state's own treasury, collected the state's revenues inside the state's own territory and remitted them abroad.

And the Ottoman Empire went on existing for another forty-one years. Its sultan still reigned; its ambassadors still presented credentials; its formal standing as a sovereign power survived intact until 1922. What had migrated was not the standing but the substance beneath it: control over the revenue streams that made the standing mean anything. The late Qing went down a parallel road. The indemnity loans China took after its defeat by Japan in 1895 were secured on the revenues of the Maritime Customs, an agency already administered by foreign officials, and the foreign-loan system extended that authority to the salt revenues as well. The dynasty's creditors were not lending to the dynasty so much as to a revenue stream that foreigners already controlled.

The Collapse Signature research names this pattern slow fiscal hollowing, and the Ottoman case is its clearest specimen. Its lesson for the present is counterintuitive. When formal standing outlasts market belief for a long time, the gap usually does not close with a crash. It closes by relocation. The institution keeps its title, and its creditors take its keys.

The empire kept its sultan, its embassies, and its seat among the powers. Its creditors kept the salt, the tobacco, and the tax collectors. Standing survived for forty-one years. Sovereignty had already left. — Slow fiscal hollowing
Chapter IV

The Decree Written in Advance

Here the historical comparison stops being an analogy and starts doing work.

The Ottoman creditors seized specific revenue streams after a default, through a negotiated decree, six years after the suspension of payments. The architecture of the AI capital web does something structurally similar, but it does it in advance, as ordinary financial engineering, before anything has gone wrong.

Consider how Project Jupiter is built. Oracle does not own the New Mexico data center. A Blue Owl unit develops it, a syndicate of banks lends $18 billion against it, and the loan is repaid from Oracle's lease payments. The same pattern recurs across the buildout: banks led by JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, SMBC, and MUFG have spent months working to distribute $38 billion of construction debt tied to data centers leased to Oracle, as this series noted in №26. The roughly $260 billion of lease commitments S&P counts in Oracle's adjusted debt is the same structure at scale. Each vehicle ring-fences a specific stream of Oracle's future payments and assigns it to a specific set of creditors.

This is not sinister, and it is not unusual. Project finance has worked this way for a century, and it lets a company build far faster than its own balance sheet would allow. But read through the Ottoman lens, its meaning changes. The Decree of Muharrem took twenty-seven years of borrowing and a default to produce: a creditor-administered claim on named revenues, insulated from the sovereign's general fortunes. The vehicle architecture produces the same claim on the day each loan closes. The creditors do not need to negotiate their way into control of the revenue stream after a crisis. They were handed it at signing.

That is why the HSBC sale is the most informative price in this story, more informative than the swap. A swap prices Oracle in general. The Jupiter loan prices one ring-fenced stream of Oracle's obligations, attached to one building, assigned to one syndicate, and the bank that knew it best chose to accept 85 cents rather than hold it. The market is no longer pricing only the standing. It is pricing the pledged revenue, the way London priced the Ottoman salt monopoly and the Chinese customs, and finding it worth less than par.

The implication for how the gap closes is specific. If formal standing and market belief stay apart for long, the Ottoman pattern predicts that control will keep migrating, quietly, toward the holders of the pledged streams: more equity issued to defend the rating, more obligations moved into vehicles, more of the company's future cash already spoken for before it arrives. Oracle would remain investment grade on paper, and a growing share of what that grade describes would already belong to someone else. No crash is required for that outcome. That is what makes it worth watching.

◆ What this chapter does not claim

It does not claim that Oracle is the Ottoman Empire, that its lenders resemble nineteenth-century bondholders in their politics, or that a default is coming. It claims a structural resemblance in one respect only: in both cases, the instruments that let creditors claim specific revenue streams directly are a measure of how far effective control has moved away from the entity that holds the formal standing. In 1881 those instruments had to be imposed. In 2026 they are standard terms. Confidence in the comparison is the project's level D: analytical inference, not evidence.

Chapter V

A Prediction, Scored

In May, the sixteenth dispatch in this series, The Self-Strengthening Trap, made a timestamped prediction: that Oracle would keep its investment-grade credit access through the first half of 2026. The publication has said from the beginning that a framework is only worth something if it can be checked, so here is the check.

The prediction held. Through June 30, Oracle kept its ratings, kept issuing debt, and kept its access to the investment-grade market. The S&P downgrade came on July 9, nine days after the window closed, and even then it stopped one notch short of junk. On the narrow terms in which it was stated, the call was correct.

On the broader terms the dispatch was gesturing at, the honest scoring is mixed. The prediction tracked the formal standing, and the formal standing held. It did not anticipate how far market belief would move while the standing held: a swap spread that roughly quintupled in a year, a bank loan trading at 85. If the May dispatch was measuring the right instrument, the instrument turned out to be the slow one. This dispatch is, in part, the correction: the variable worth tracking was never whether the rating would hold. It was how far the price could drift from it while it did.

There is also the case for the agencies, which the series should state at full strength. Barclays' Jigar Patel has called downgrades across the AI-linked names "not a near-term worry." Vanguard's Brad Collins has argued that stress at the speculative end of the market should not be read across to investment-grade issuers that are, after all, still paying. Oracle's contracted revenue is enormous, its existing businesses throw off real cash, and management has shown it will sell stock to defend creditors. Swap markets overshoot. They are thinner than bond markets, they are used for hedging as much as for judgment, and the whole AI complex has been repricing together, which means some of Oracle's spread is simply the sector's mood. It is entirely possible that in a year the gap will have closed because the market came back toward the agencies, and this dispatch will have been an essay about a false alarm.

That is why the falsification section below matters more than any argument above it. The gap will close. The direction it closes in will say which reading was right.

Falsification

Falsification

The rating-price gap can close in three ways. Each points to a different historical pattern, and each is observable.

The agencies move toward the market. Two of the three agencies cut Oracle below investment grade, or Moody's resolves its negative outlook with a downgrade that brings the middle rating to the edge → the gap closes from the agencies' side, the faster historical pattern is in play, and the slow-hollowing reading in Chapter IV is wrong about tempo.

The market moves toward the agencies. Oracle's five-year swap returns below roughly 150 basis points and Oracle-linked loans trade back near par, without any downgrade → the market overshot, the signature misfired on its first public test, and Chapters I through IV overread a sector-wide repricing.

The gap persists while control migrates. Ratings hold, swaps stay wide, and the evidence accumulates on the creditor side: further loan sales below par, more equity issued to defend the rating, more obligations moved into named vehicles → the Ottoman pattern, slow fiscal hollowing, is the operative one, and this dispatch's central claim holds.

The first data point arrives soon. Oracle's fiscal second quarter closes November 30, and the company reported the same quarter on December 10 last year. Watch the free cash flow line, the capital spending guidance, and the progress of the $20 billion equity program. The series will score this dispatch formally at the end of the second quarter of 2027, against all three outcomes, whichever way they fall.

Investment grade on paper. Single-B in the market. Eighty-five cents in the loan book. The rating is not wrong because it is slow. It is slow because it is holding something up. The question is not whether the standing survives. It is who owns what lies beneath it by the time anyone checks.
